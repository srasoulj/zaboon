# Hello & ta'ārof

In this unit you'll greet people, say thank you and take your first steps in ta'ārof, the Persian
art of politeness. Maman Bozorg will offer you tea (more than once), and Shirin will teach you what
people really say on the streets of Tehran.

## Spoken first, written second

Persian is spoken one way and written another. Zaboon teaches the everyday Tehrani form first.
When the written form is different, it appears in a small "written" chip under the sentence:

| Spoken                                        | Written                                                         | Meaning            |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| <fa audio="">خوبی؟</fa> khubi?                | <fa audio="">خوب هستی؟</fa> khub hasti?                         | Are you well?      |
| <fa audio="">مرسی، خوبم.</fa> mersi, khubam.  | <fa audio="">متشکرم، خوب هستم.</fa> motashakkeram, khub hastam. | Thanks, I'm well.  |
| <fa audio="">اسمم شیرینه.</fa> esmam shirine. | <fa audio="">اسم من شیرین است.</fa> esm-e man shirin ast.       | My name is Shirin. |

Both are correct, and your answers are accepted in either form.

## Two ways to say "you"

Use <fa audio="">تو</fa> (to) with friends, family and children, and <fa audio="">شما</fa> (shomā)
with elders, strangers and anyone you want to show respect. The verb changes too: ask a friend
<fa audio="">خوبی؟</fa> (khubi?), but ask Maman Bozorg <fa audio="">شما خوبین؟</fa> (shomā
khubin?). When in doubt, choose <fa audio="">شما</fa>.

## Ta'ārof

Ta'ārof is a little dance of offering, refusing and insisting. When Maman Bozorg says
<fa audio="">بفرمایید چایی!</fa> ("Have some tea!"), it's polite to say <fa audio="">نه، مرسی.</fa>
the first time. She will insist: <fa audio="">تعارف نکن!</fa> ("Don't be shy!"). Now you can accept:
<fa audio="">باشه، مرسی!</fa>

You'll meet the same dance in shops and taxis. If the driver says <fa audio="">قابل نداره</fa>
(ghābel nadāre, "it's nothing, be my guest"), that's ta'ārof too: thank him and pay anyway.

## Reading right to left

Persian is written from right to left, so a sentence starts at the right-hand edge. Most short
vowels aren't written: <fa audio="">من</fa> is just m-n, and you add the "a" of man yourself. That's
why every word shows its transliteration until you know its letters. Start the Letters tab with
five letters that never join the letter after them: <fa audio="">ا د ر ز و</fa>.
