# About me

You'll introduce yourself and talk about where you're from with Kian and Shirin. Kian is a heritage speaker: he speaks some Persian but can't read it yet.

## Two ways to say “I am”

After a consonant, you can attach the ending -am: <fa audio="">من کیانم.</fa> (man kiānam.) means “I'm Kian.” The same ending appears in <fa audio="">اهل تهرانم.</fa> (ahl-e tehrānam.), “I'm from Tehran.” You don't need a separate word for “am” in these examples.

After a vowel, this unit uses the separate word <fa audio="">هستم</fa> (hastam). Compare <fa audio="">من ایرانی هستم.</fa> (man irāni hastam.) and <fa audio="">آره، دانشجو هستم.</fa> (āre, dāneshju hastam.). This is a useful starting pattern, not the only possible way to phrase introductions.

For “I'm not,” use <fa audio="">نیستم</fa> (nistam), as in Kian and Shirin's conversation about nationality.

## Where are you from?

Ask a friend <fa audio="">اهل کجایی؟</fa> (ahl-e kojāyi?). For a respectful question, choose <fa audio="">شما اهل کجایین؟</fa> (shomā ahl-e kojāyin?). Both ask about someone's origins, which may differ from where they live now.

Answer with <fa audio="">اهل تهرانم.</fa> (ahl-e tehrānam.), and use <fa audio="">خوشبختم.</fa> (khoshbakhtam.) when meeting someone. It means “Nice to meet you,” not a general greeting for every encounter.

## Same spelling, different sound

You'll see <fa audio="">تو</fa> pronounced to for “you” and tu for “in.” In <fa audio="">تو دانشجو هستی؟</fa> (to dāneshju hasti?), it means “you.” In <fa audio="">تو تهران زندگی می‌کنم.</fa> (tu tehrān zendegi mikonam.), it means “in.” Listen to the vowel and use the surrounding words for help.

Like Kian, you can explain your limits: <fa audio="">یه کم فارسی حرف می‌زنم.</fa> (ye kam fārsi harf mizanam.). If you're lost, <fa audio="">ببخشید، نمی‌فهمم.</fa> (bebakhshid, nemifahmam.) keeps the conversation honest and friendly.
