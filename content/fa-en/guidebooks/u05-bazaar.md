# Numbers & the bazaar

You'll practice counting and asking prices with Leila, then hear Dariush's polite response when it's time to pay. You'll also learn how to ask for a discount without confusing courtesy with a free purchase.

## Count it out

Practice the three number rows aloud to count from one to ten. Listen especially for Tehrani <fa audio="">چهار</fa> (chār), “four,” and <fa audio="">شش</fa> (shish), “six.” Their everyday pronunciation may differ from what you've heard in formal Persian.

<fa audio="">نه</fa> (noh) means “nine.” The same spelling, pronounced na, means “no”: the vowel makes the difference.

When counting individual items, put <fa audio="">تا</fa> (tā) between the number and the noun. In <fa audio="">دو تا چایی، لطفا.</fa> (do tā chāyi, lotfan.), it helps you count two servings of tea. The noun doesn't need an English-style plural ending. For a measured amount, use the unit instead: <fa audio="">دو کیلو برنج می‌خرم.</fa> (do kilo berenj mikharam.) means “I'll buy two kilos of rice.”

## Ask the price

Use <fa audio="">این چنده؟</fa> (in chande?) for an item's price and <fa audio="">کیلویی چنده؟</fa> (kiloyi chande?) for the price per kilogram.

Prices are usually spoken in tomans: one toman equals ten rials. Official written amounts may use rials, so check the currency. The written word is <fa audio="">تومان</fa> (tomān), but people say and text <fa audio="">تومن</fa> (toman).

React with <fa audio="">خیلی گرونه!</fa> (kheyli gerune!), or hear <fa audio="">این ارزونه.</fa> (in arzune.), “This is inexpensive.”

## Bargain, then pay

Where bargaining is appropriate, ask <fa audio="">تخفیف می‌دین؟</fa> (takhfif midin?). <fa audio="">آخرش چند؟</fa> (ākharesh chand?) asks for the final or best price.

A seller or driver may say <fa audio="">قابل نداره!</fa> (ghābel nadāre!), literally “It's nothing.” That's often ta'ārof, not a waived bill. Thank them and pay anyway; <fa audio="">خواهش می‌کنم، بفرمایید.</fa> (khāhesh mikonam, befarmāyid.) politely accompanies handing over your payment.
