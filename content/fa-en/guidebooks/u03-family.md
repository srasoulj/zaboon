# Family

You'll visit Maman Bozorg with Kian and talk about the people in your family. You'll introduce relatives, ask about siblings, and hear some warm, everyday expressions of affection.

## Little endings, close connections

Persian often attaches possession to the end of a noun. The endings -am, -et, and -esh mean “my,” “your,” and “his/her.” Listen for -et in <fa audio="">خواهرت</fa> (khāharet), “your sister,” and -esh in <fa audio="">اسمش</fa> (esmesh), “his/her name.” The last ending doesn't tell you the person's gender.

In <fa audio="">این مامانمه.</fa> (in māmāname.), the ending for “my” is followed by the ending for “is”: “This is my mom.” Treat it as a useful introduction pattern. <fa audio="">این بابامه.</fa> (in bābāme.) works the same way for your dad.

## Who has siblings?

For “have,” learn these forms together: <fa audio="">دارم</fa> (dāram), “I have”; <fa audio="">داری</fa> (dāri), “you have”; and <fa audio="">داره</fa> (dāre), “he/she has.”

Ask <fa audio="">خواهر داری؟</fa> (khāhar dāri?) and answer <fa audio="">آره، یه خواهر دارم.</fa> (āre, ye khāhar dāram.). To make “I have” negative, use <fa audio="">ندارم</fa> (nadāram): <fa audio="">برادر ندارم.</fa> (barādar nadāram.). No separate word corresponding to English “don't” is needed.

## At Grandma's

<fa audio="">خانواده‌ت خوبن؟</fa> (khānevādat khuban?) asks after the whole family. When saying goodbye, <fa audio="">به مامانت سلام برسون!</fa> (be māmānet salām beresun!) passes along a greeting.

Grandma addresses Kian with familiar “you,” while Kian uses respectful <fa audio="">شما</fa> (shomā) with her. Warmth and respect can go together.

<fa audio="">عزیزم</fa> (azizam) means “my dear.” <fa audio="">قربونت برم، عزیزم!</fa> (ghorbunet beram, azizam!) includes the literal wish “may I be sacrificed for you.” It's an everyday loving expression, not a literal offer: understand it as “I adore you, sweetheart!”
